Part 1: New England and Middle Colonies

New England Colonies

  • The New England Colonies include Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Connecticut, and New Hampshire
  • Massachusetts
    • Plymouth Settled 1620 by Separatist Puritans
      • Via Holland
      • Received charter to settle northern Virginia
      • Began on 2 ships, all eventually sailed in Mayflower
      • Arrived off New England coast 1620
        • 102 aboard ship
        • One died, one born at sea
        • Discarded original destination plans on arrival
        • Would settle in New England without permission
      • Signed Mayflower Compact before disembarking
        • Signed by 41 adult males
          • (not servants and 2 seamen)
        • Agreement to form a crude government (“Body Politic”)
          • Would submit to the will of the majority
        • Self-government
          • Open discussion town meetings
        • Hardship
          • Only 44 survived the winter of 1620-1621
        • Prosperous spring and summer 1621
          • Fur, fish, lumber
        • Bountiful harvests autumn 1621
          • First Thanksgiving Day in New England
    • Massachusetts Bay
      • Settled 1630 by non-separatist Puritans
      • Received charter from the King
        • Took the charter with them to Massachusetts, transferring authority from England to America
      • Arrived in 11 ships carrying 1000 settlers
      • Prospered: trading, fishing, shipbuilding
      • First Governor John Winthrop
      • Believed had covenant with God
        • to build holy society
        • to be a model for humankind
      • Government
        • All freemen had right to vote
        • Town governments: male property-owners voted
        • Government’s duty: enforce religious rules
        • Clergymen barred from holding public office
    • Massachusetts Bay dissenters
      • Quakers
        • Fines, flogging, banishment
      • Anne Hutchinson
        • Antinomianism
        • Banished
      • Roger Williams
        • Wanted separation from Church of England
        • Condemned taking Indian land
        • Denied government authority to regulate religious behavior
  • Rhode Island
    • Founded by Roger Williams in 1636
      • After being driven out of Massachusetts and wintering with Indians
      • Haven for those seeking religious freedom
      • Received government charter from Parliament 1644
      • Roger Williams: Founding of Rhode Island
  • Connecticut
    • Founded 1636 by Thomas Hooker
      • Driven out of Massachusetts
      • Obtained royal charter
      • Adopted Fundamental Orders of Connecticut
        • First constitution in present USA
  • New Hampshire
      • Founded 1638 by John Wheelwright
        • Banished from Boston
      • Began as proprietary colony, later became a Royal colony
  • New England Colonies Commonalities
    • Economy
      • Self-sufficient (kitchen crop and plot outside town for grain)
      • Lumber, manufacturing, small businesses
      • Fur, fish, cotton, sugar, rice, wine
    • Literacy (male): 80-90%

Middle Colonies

  • The Middle colonies are New York, New Jersey, Delaware, And Pennsylvania
  • New York
    • Established 1626
    • Originally New Netherlands
    • Claimed by Charles II 1664, granted to Duke of York
    • Dutch forced to surrender
    • Originally proprietary, became Royal 1685
  • Delaware
    • 1631 Dutch settlement failed
    • 1638 New Sweden founded
      • Dutch regained control 1655
      • English gained control 1664
      • 1662 awarded to William Penn
      • Proprietary colony
  • Pennsylvania
    • Established 1681
      • Charles II land grant to William Penn
        • Society of Friends
      • Established for Quakers and other refuges
      • “no lawsuits”
      • Laws stressed rehabilitation
      • Proprietary colony
      • Settlers: Dutch, Scots, Irish, Germans, English                            
  • New Jersey
    • Established 1664
    • Proprietary grant by Duke of York to Sir George Carteret and Lord Berkeley
    • Became royal Colony 1772

Middle Colonies’ Commonalities

  • Fertile Soil, broad expanse of land
  • Rivers and streams suitable for navigation
  • Ethnically mixed population
  • Virgin forests
  • Industries flourished
    • Fur trade with the interior
    • Lumbering, Shipbuilding, dairy products, pork, cattle
    • Trade/seaports
    • Most prosperous colonial region
    • Literacy (male) 60%

Part 2: Colonial Life

  • English colonists experienced various levels of liberty according to their status Links to an external site..

Servitude

  • Indentured Servants
    • Colonists who exchanged years of labor for cost of passage to America
    • Hopes of eventual land grant
      • Between 1610 and 1775, more than 350,000 of the 500,000 immigrants arrived as indentured servants
    • Primary source of labor in English colonial period
      • Especially for tobacco planters
    • Included homeless London children
    • Included more than 30,000 convicts
    • Had limited rights and protections
    • Claimed “freedom dues” when servitude ended
      • Tools, clothing, food
      • Sometimes small plot of land or cash
  • Chattel (lifetime) slavery, primarily of Africans, became widespread in Colonial America in the 1660s, becoming a part of American culture that would forever blot its history.
    • Barbados Slave Codes
      • British Laws passed in 1661
      • justified slavery
      • legalized inhumane treatment of slaves
      • Slaves had the status of property
      • and had no human rights
  • Women enjoyed limited rights in the 13 English American colonies, especially according to written law. From birth they were considered under the jurisdiction of their fathers until they married and came under the jurisdiction of their husbands. However, harsh conditions and reality often intervened to put women in positions of authority since their fathers and husbands frequently relied on them to take care of business in their absence. Women in Puritan-influenced New England had fewer rights as dictated by their religious traditions; however, women of the Middle and Southern Colonies women were given many more responsibilities and rights than women in England. Women and free black property owners in New Jersey even exercised the right to vote between 1776 and 1807 when the state legislature "liberalized" voting rights by abolishing the property ownership requirement and defining eligible voters as "white male taxpayers."  Visit the following Virtual Exhibits for more on the expansion and retraction of voting rights for New Jersey women and blacks.

  • Apprentices were young people, usually young men, whose parents had signed an agreement putting them under the jurisdiction of a skilled tradesman, considered their master, for a period of time, usually five to seven years, in return for training in that skill. The master retained the legal right to punish the apprentice as he saw fit and the apprentice contract could be enforced by law.

 

Colonial Anomalies

  • Anthony Johnson
    • 1621 Arrived in Virginia
    • Referred to as "Antonio a Negro" in early records
    • Anthony went to work on a tobacco plantation
      • indentured servant or slave
    • 1622 survived Good Friday Powhatan raid
      • 355 colonists killed
        • 52 dead, 5 survived on Plantation where Johnson worked
      • 1625 Johnson married “Mary a Negro”
        • Had four children
          • 1640s Johnsons raised livestock and farmed their own place
        • Johnson property had grown to 250 acres in 1650s
        • Johnsons sons acquired property at adulthood
          • John and Richard
        • Johnson purchased one or more slaves to work property
        • Anthony Johnson and family moved to Maryland 1665
          • Leased 300 acres
          • Sold 250 acre Virginia property
          • Took 14 head of cattle and 8 sheep
        • Anthony Johnson died 1670
        • Mary extended lease 99 years
        • Virginia court ruled that Johnson’s former Virginia property reverted to “The Crown”
        • No further record of Johnson’s sons or heirs is available at this time although free blacks with the Johnson surname appear in late 17th and early 18th century Virginia records
  • Benjamin Franklin
        • Born in Boston 1706
        • 1718 Apprenticed to brother James to work in his print shop
        • Forced to sign on for 12 year apprenticeship
        • Franklin resented the disciplinary beatings rendered to him by his Master/brother
          • Physical punishment for apprentices, servants, wives, and children such as beatings, chains, confinement, and withholding food were customary and legal in British Colonial America
        • Apprenticeship contracts were enforceable by law, so Franklin ran away booking secret passage to New York in 1723
          • Franklin relocated to Philadelphia where he eventually opened his own print shop after several years of work, travel, and adventure
  • Eliza Lucas Pinckney
        • Born in West Indies and educated in England, Arrived in South Carolina in 1734
        • Left in charge of her ailing mother, young sister, and 3 plantations at age 16 when her father was called back into military service in the Caribbean
        • Kept a letter book record of all her correspondence as she informed her father of business, political, and family matters
        • Developed cultivation of indigo which became a large cash crop for Carolina and the British Colonies
        • Later married Charles Pinckney, continued to take part in the operations of his plantations, and raised three children including two sons who fought in the American Revolution. One son was also a signer of the Constitution.

Now on exhibit at the Smithsonian Museum of American History, this is Eliza Lucas Pinckney's dress made from silk grown, processed, and woven on her plantation under her supervision.
